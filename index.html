Jan Soochna Portal: A Case Study in Co-Designing Government Transparency

How a three-year collaboration between civil society and the Government of Rajasthan produced India's most comprehensive proactive disclosure platform, serving 230M+ citizens across 350+ welfare schemes.

Khush Vachhrajani  |  Independent Consultant  |  Governance & Service Design

The Problem

Citizens had legal rights to information. Government systems made it impossible to access.

Section 4 of India's Right to Information Act legally mandates that all public authorities proactively disclose 16 categories of information including subsidy recipients, service details, and expenditure records. A 2018 audit by the Central Information Commission found that only 4% of surveyed central authorities were complying. A separate study found that 67% of RTI applications filed by citizens were seeking information that should already have been public.

The consequence was not administrative. It was human.

Pensions

In Bhim Block alone, 3,729 pensions were stopped. Over 1,000 people were incorrectly certified as dead by government systems. Many died waiting for restoration because they had no way to find out why payments stopped or who had made that decision.

Healthcare

A woman in Kaladeh was charged Rs. 15,000 by a private hospital for surgery that was free under a government insurance scheme. The hospital claimed it had not received the insurance payment. Citizens had no way to verify or dispute this without portal data.

Rations

In one panchayat in Bhilwara, a dealer recorded online that rations had been delivered. They had not been. Citizens had no evidence to prove the fraud. Over 5,300kg of grain had been siphoned off.

RTI applications were too slow. By the time information arrived, the harm was already done. The system needed real-time, transaction-level disclosure.

The Process

Three years of structured negotiation between activists and government officials.

The Jan Soochna Portal did not emerge from a government IT department. It emerged from a co-design process initiated in September 2016 between the SR Abhiyan, a coalition of nearly 80 civil society organisations and unions, and the Government of Rajasthan's Department of Information Technology and Communication.

The core mechanism was a series of Digital Dialogues: bi-monthly structured meetings where grassroots activists and government IT officials worked through, scheme by scheme, what data existed in government systems and what should be made public.

2005–2016

SR Abhiyan campaigns on right to work and right to information across Rajasthan. Yatras, public hearings, and RTI campaigns reveal systematic information gaps and their human cost.

September 2016

First Digital Dialogue convened. Civil society forms an internal JIS Group: grassroots activists with scheme-level expertise in health, education, labour, and social security.

2016–2019

Study phase. The JIS Group is given access to government administrator logins to see the full scope of data collected versus what was disclosed publicly. Scheme-by-scheme negotiations begin.

Ongoing

For each of 48+ schemes, the JIS Group produces detailed format specifications: exact rows, columns, and data fields. Any government refusal to disclose requires a written justification citing the specific RTI exemption clause.

September 13, 2019

Jan Soochna Portal launched by the Chief Minister of Rajasthan. The dialogues have continued since. The volume of schemes and information has grown significantly in the years following launch.

My Role in This Work

Phase 2: expanding the portal from 21 schemes to hundreds, and taking disclosure into communities.

I joined this work in July 2020 as my first assignment after completing my Master of Public Affairs at Brown University. The portal had launched in September 2019 with 21 schemes. My role in Phase 2 was to expand and deepen it: coordinating between civil society JIS teams and government departments, co-designing data formats for new disclosure areas, convening and facilitating Digital Dialogues, and designing the approaches to take information disclosed on the portal into communities and bring their feedback and grievances back into the design process.

The portal now covers 353 schemes across 117 departments and has been accessed by 298 million people.

Schemes I worked on

Social security pensions, COVID-19 relief and ex-gratia compensation payments, Building and Other Construction Workers Act disclosures, and mining-related disclosures. I also worked with the Office of the Chief Secretary and the Secretary of IT to draft and issue guidelines for the portal, grounded in social accountability principles and the experience of the dialogues.

Community outreach and feedback

After disclosure, I worked with people's campaigns, social movements, and unions to ensure information was actively used. This included community meetings and night camps where I projected the portal onto the walls of schools and health centres using a portable projector, walking communities through what was now visible and how to use it. Their feedback and unresolved grievances were then brought back into the next dialogue to strengthen both disclosure and grievance redress.

Facilitating the dialogues

I convened and facilitated multiple Digital Dialogues with civil society representatives (35 to 50 participants), line departments (up to 8 to 10 at a time), and the state IT department. The dialogues were deliberately open: the wider the civil society participation, the stronger the shared ownership of the outcomes across both activists and bureaucrats.

What I would do differently

The portal's structure is difficult to navigate on a mobile phone, which is how most users access it. Online-only disclosure is also insufficient. I proposed and designed an on-ground facilitation model at the village level where people would be assisted in reading the information, sharing feedback, and using it to seek accountability. That infrastructure was laid out clearly but stalled due to a lack of administrative and political will. If I were designing it again, I would insist on the facilitation layer being built in from the start, not treated as a separate implementation problem.

Three questions that anchored every negotiation

  1. How will this information help the most marginalised access their entitlements?
  2. How will this information increase transparency and accountability of the system?
  3. How will this information strengthen participation of the most marginalised in governance?

A Design Artifact: COVID-19 Relief Disclosure Formats

During the COVID-19 lockdown, I led the co-design of disclosure formats for three channels of state relief: cash transfers to families who lost earning members to COVID-19, dry ration distribution through Anganwadis and schools, and cash transfers to informal workers including street vendors, construction workers, and rickshaw drivers who lost livelihoods during the lockdown.

The first step was forming a JIS team of civil society volunteers working across these three areas. Together, we met with six line departments including Finance, Labour Welfare, Food and Civil Supplies, Women and Child Development, Education, and Local Self Government to map their existing data systems: what was digitised, to what granularity, and how frequently it was updated.

The formats below were the output of that process. Each column was negotiated. The government's primary resistance was political: COVID deaths, hunger, and livelihood collapse were sensitive. I anchored those negotiations. The government eventually not only disclosed the requested information but created a dedicated grievance workflow for COVID-related complaints arising from the disclosure. A helpline ran for one year from the date the information went live.

Designing the Data

Every format was a negotiation. Every column was a decision about whose interests the data would serve.

The most significant design work was not visual. It was structural. The JIS Group negotiated, scheme by scheme, not just what data would be disclosed but at what level of granularity, in what format, and organised by what geography.

Health Services

Activists insisted on institutional-level disclosure rather than state-wide aggregates. Negotiated formats include medicine stock levels by clinic, functional toilets per facility, and names of beneficiaries of maternal health payments so communities can verify disbursement.

E-Mitra Kiosks

To address corruption at service kiosks, the format includes the exact amount charged per transaction and the commission earned by the operator. Citizens can compare what they were asked to pay against the government record.

Mining

Information is structured at the individual lease level. Formats include daily dispatch records, names and demographics of workers employed, and a log of all reported accidents and deaths at each site.

Ration (PDS)

When the state moved to biometric authentication, the format was updated to show NFSA beneficiary entitlements alongside what was actually received. This allowed citizens to produce printable evidence of siphoning.

Three guiding principles of format design

Geographic first: Information is organised by Panchayat or Municipal Ward. A citizen selects their location and sees everything relevant to their community.

Transaction-level: Data is pulled in real time from the same databases used by government officials, not from static reports.

Actionable by design: Every row and column was tested against a single question: can this help a citizen identify a rights violation or hold a specific official accountable?

What Changed

Specific cases where portal data produced accountability.

The portal's impact is measured not in page views but in recoveries. Below are documented cases where citizens used portal data to establish and enforce their rights.

Rations, Bhilwara

Citizens in Thana Panchayat used portal data showing online records of deliveries they never received. The ration dealer was suspended. Over 5,300kg of grain was recovered and distributed. A similar confrontation in Katar Panchayat resulted in the recovery of three truckloads of stolen wheat.

Health Insurance, Kaladeh

Sohni Devi was charged Rs. 15,000 by a private hospital for surgery that should have been free. Activists used the portal to show the hospital the exact date and amount of the insurance disbursement. The hospital returned the money.

Automated Welfare, State-wide

Portal data integration enabled policy shifts toward automation. In November 2017, over 240,000 eligible citizens were added to the National Food Security Act list with a single administrative action. In July 2018, the state used its database to automatically update records for over 900,000 pensioners to receive an increased widow pension rate, with no physical application required.

Labour Fraud, Beawar

A social audit using portal data uncovered a Rs. 2 lakh insurance fraud. An operator had registered a deceased homemaker as a construction worker to claim a death benefit. The portal's audit trail was specific enough that the person listed as employer admitted publicly he was a tailor, not a contractor.

Water Access, Choti Kadeen

Residents filed a water crisis complaint during the COVID-19 lockdown. When the portal showed the complaint had been marked as resolved despite no action, they used a printout of that status to file a second complaint. The District Collector ordered daily water tanker deliveries until a permanent solution was built.
